Wind limits & reefing

Bottom line: the Puck is a sheltered-waters trainer, certified CE Category D — designed for wind up to Force 4 (~16 knots) and waves up to 0.3 m. Full rig through Force 4; if it builds into Force 5, put the single reef in and keep youth crews ashore; don't launch above Force 6 (~27 knots). It has been sailed harder in experienced hands, but Force 4 is the certified design limit and a sensible ceiling for the day. For a young or light crew, play it safer — reef earlier and come ashore sooner.

The reef point

The Puck carries one reef in the mainsail — a single reef is mandatory in the class rules, set at least 1 metre up the luff — and that reef is the boat's main tool for rising wind. There is no manufacturer knot figure of the kind RS gives for the Quest (reef at 18 knots); what is certified is the Category D envelope (Force 4), and what the class documents is the handling — the boat heels very little even at Force 5 and has been sailed across Force 1–6. A sensible working rule for youth sailing: full rig to Force 4, and the reef in by Force 5 (roughly 18–21 knots). The reef is cheap insurance and goes in before launching, not after.

Built for this water

The Puck was designed in 1996 at the Scout Maritime Centre (Harcerski Ośrodek Morski) in Puck, on Puck Bay — a bay that is not an easy stretch of water, with a periodically changing state. It was made to take those conditions where the club's old Omegas could not. Trials ran in Force 1–6, with the prevailing N–NW wind around Force 4 and waves of 40–60 cm. The adjustable centreboard (0.20–1.05 m) lets it work shallow water, and the rudder and centreboard are built solid for reliability. In short: it is a boat that expects a working breeze and a training crew, and forgives both.

Extra caution for youth & light crews

The Puck is certified for a maximum of 6 persons and 480 kg total load. A light or young crew reaches the point of being overpowered before a heavier crew does, because they cannot sit the boat down as hard — so they should reef earlier and come ashore sooner as it builds. Match the sail to the crew's weight and confidence, not to a fixed number, and keep an instructor's eye on the gusts.

Stability & the unsinkable hull

Two things make the Puck a safe training boat. First, stiffness: it heels very little even in Force 5, which is what makes it forgiving when a novice freezes or lets a sheet fly — "even if someone lets go of the sheets, nothing bad happens and the Puck copes," as the class puts it. Second, it is unsinkable: the polyester-glass hull has built-in buoyancy and closable fore and aft peaks, so a swamped or capsized boat floats and can be recovered. Keep the boat flat, ease early, and the boat's own design does the rest.

The certified envelope

The Puck is certified CE design Category D — sheltered waters: wind up to Force 4 and significant wave height up to 0.3 m (occasionally to 0.5 m). That is the formal envelope, certified by Poland's PRS (Polski Rejestr Statków, notified body 1463) to EN ISO 12217-3:2017 — the small-craft stability and buoyancy standard — under report CW/RCD/3350/2020 (August 2020). The certificate also fixes the load: maximum 6 persons and 480 kg total, on a 230 kg glass-reinforced- polyester hull. The Martwa Wisła at the Jamboree Marina sits well inside Category D — sheltered, flat water. As with any training dinghy, the limit in practice is crew capability and capsize, not the hull.

About the class

The Puck is the Polish scouting movement's own training boat — designed by shipwright Franciszek Lewiński, owned by ZHP (Polish Scouting), and sailed in the hundreds across Poland. New to it? There's a plain-English introduction on the What is a Puck? page.
