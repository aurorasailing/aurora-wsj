Wind limits & reefing

Bottom line: full main to about 15 knots for a competent crew; reef by 18; furl the jib and sail on reefed main alone above that. Treat Force 6 — roughly 27 knots — as the edge of the boat's designed world, not a target.

Quick reference

WindSail planAction
Up to ~15 ktFull main + jibSail flat; weight outboard as it builds; spinnaker only within crew skill.
15–18 ktFull main, depoweredEase the gnav, flatten the sails; light or youth crews reef now.
18 ktReef the mainFirst slab in before launching — the manufacturer's figure.
18–~22 ktReefed main; jib as neededFurl the jib if overpowered; no spinnaker.
Force 6 (~22–27 kt)Reefed main onlyExperienced adult crew only; youth crews ashore.
Above Force 6 (>27 kt)—Outside the certified envelope; do not launch.

Bands are indicative and assume open water. For a light or inexperienced crew, play it safer — reef earlier and come in sooner.

The reef point

RS Sailing's own Sailing Tips give one hard number: reef the mainsail at 18 knots before going to sea. Experienced crews may take a reef in afloat as the breeze builds, but 18 knots is the figure the boat is designed around — below it, full main; at or above it, the first slab goes in before launching.

Extra caution for youth & light crews

Maximum crew weight is 365 kg — about four adults; no minimum is published. A light youth crew reaches the reef threshold well before 18 knots and should reef earlier, because they cannot sit the boat down. Match sail to crew weight rather than to a fixed number.

The de-power sequence

RS gives a clear order for rising wind:

  • Move crew weight outboard and ease the gnav to spill the top of the main.
  • Reef the mainsail at 18 knots.
  • In stronger conditions, furl the jib and progressively ease the gnav while keeping steerage.

RS is blunt about the boat: large, heavy, three sails, a high centre of effort. It wants an experienced skipper, and over-powering it may end in a capsize.

Main only — never jib only

Main alone is a proper reduced rig: the forestay is fixed and the jib removable, and the boat sails well balanced under main only. Jib alone is not a configuration for this boat — it gives a large lee helm and poor control. In a blow you keep a reefed main and furl the jib, never the reverse.

Capsize & recovery

On capsize in strong wind, furl the jib before righting to give the boat weather helm. The foam-filled masthead section resists inversion, and a masthead float kit is available for crews sailing near the limit. Two features help lighter crews recover: handles moulded into the hull, and scuppers under the bench seats that take on water when the boat is over, so it sits lower on its side and the centreboard is easier to reach — the water drains out again once the boat is upright.

The fragile end is the rudder

The one repeated field warning: do not load the tiller sideways — it damages the lift-and-lock mechanism. The blade kicks up on grounding (which protects it at the slipway); the strain to watch is sailing overpowered with heavy weather helm, which loads the stock and tiller. Keep the boat flat and the load stays off it. The hull itself — rotomoulded Comptec PE3 — outlasts the crew's ability to keep it upright.

The certified envelope

The RS Quest is CE design Category C: winds up to Force 6 (22–27 kt) and significant wave height up to 2 m. That is the extent of what "tested" means. No structural-failure-by-windspeed data is published for the Quest — or for training dinghies generally — because the limiting factor is capsize and crew capability, not the hull. Treat any claim of a specific windspeed at which a fitting fails as plausible caution, not a documented figure.

From experience — the real-world edge

The certified ~27 knots is a launch and design limit, not a breaking point. In practice a reefed Quest carrying weight — a heavy crew and gear — has held together in gusts past 30 knots, keeping the boat flat, the gnav eased and steerage on. That is surviving a blow you're caught in, not a reason to launch. The launch call stays conservative — we wouldn't have gone out in it — but if you're already out and it builds: reef, ease the gnav, keep the boat flat, and head in.
